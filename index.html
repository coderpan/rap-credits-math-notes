How I’d Budget for One Short AI Rap Video Clip

I budget a short rap video by the number of paid takes I can afford. If I need four posts, I write down four finished clips, then leave room for a few takes I might discard. The subscription price alone doesn’t tell me whether that plan fits my budget.

For a standard short paid EasyMusic rap clip, I use 300 credits per take. With the monthly pool of $12.80 for 1,000 credits, the calculation is 300 ÷ 1,000 × $12.80 = $3.84. I label that about $3.8–4 per clip in my notes.

Peers are about $5+ per short clip, based on team observation. That is a rough comparison, not a verified price list for named competitors. For my own budget, the more useful question is how many paid takes I’ll need to get something I can post.

1. Write the brief before generating

I start with a sentence like this:

Birthday roast for Maya. Her name and the punchline must be clear. Vertical video.

That gives me something specific to check when I watch the result. A clip can look fine and still miss the point if nobody can hear the birthday person’s name.

Next, I read the lyrics aloud and time them. If I have to rush the last line, I shorten it before spending credits. “Everybody knows Maya always arrives an hour late” can become “Maya’s late again.” I’d rather cut six words now than pay for another take because the joke was too long.

I also decide whether I want a solo or group performance and choose a style before generating. If I’m supplying exact lyrics, I keep performance instructions separate from the words I want sung. My first draft should be short enough that I can spot an accidental instruction in the lyric box.

2. Put the credit cost beside the brief

I keep these numbers next to the script:

The $3.84 figure allocates the monthly pool price across the credits used. It does not mean I can buy a single clip at a $3.84 checkout. I check the current pricing and terms before paying, especially if I need more credits than one pool contains.

Unused credits matter to my budget too. If I pay $12.80 and use only one 300-credit take, I have spent $12.80 that month. The take used $3.84 worth of the pool, but the rest of my payment still needs to be accounted for. I don’t call those unused credits savings.

3. Give the free preview a specific job

Free Lite provides one 10-second preview at 480P with a watermark. I would use it to check whether Maya’s name and the punchline are understandable.

Before listening, I write down what I’m checking. Afterward, I record the problem in plain words: “The second line sounds rushed” or “Maya’s name is unclear.” That tells me what to edit. “Make it better” doesn’t.

One preview only tells me about that result. It cannot show how consistently the tool will handle every name or verse. A watermarked 480P preview also gives me limited evidence about the appearance of a paid output. I wouldn’t use it to promise someone a particular final result.

If I need a new paid generation after the preview, I put another 300 credits in the budget. I don’t plan around getting repeated free attempts.

4. Count takes separately from posts

For four posts with one paid take each, I need:

  • 4 × 300 = 1,200 credits
  • 4 × $3.84 = $15.36 in allocated credit cost

If each post needs a second take, that becomes:

  • 8 × 300 = 2,400 credits
  • 8 × $3.84 = $30.72 in allocated credit cost

Those are planning calculations at the monthly pool rate. They are not quotes for a 1,200-credit or 2,400-credit package. Both totals exceed a single 1,000-credit pool, so I need to check how I would obtain the extra credits and what I would actually pay.

A smaller example helps when reviewing results. Suppose I generate six paid takes and post four. I used 1,800 credits, worth $23.04 at that rate. Dividing by four gives $5.76 per posted clip. Each generation still used 300 credits; the discarded takes raised my cost per usable post.

5. Decide what deserves another take

I watch the first paid result against the brief. Can I hear the name? Does the punchline finish clearly? Does the video fit the intended post?

If the name is unclear, I can simplify the line around it before trying again. If the take meets the brief and I only feel curious about another style, I decide whether that curiosity is worth another 300 credits.

Failed paid jobs refund credits. I keep those separate from completed takes I choose not to post. For budgeting, I count a completed take I dislike as spent credits; I don’t assume that disliking the result qualifies it as a failed job.

6. Keep a short record

I use a worksheet like this:

Clip: Maya birthday roast
Must hear clearly: Maya's name, final punchline
Free Lite preview: used; second line needs shortening
Paid takes: 2
Credits: 2 × 300 = 600
Allocated cost: 600 × $0.0128 = $7.68
Posted: 1 clip
Reason for extra take: name unclear in first paid result

The $0.0128 rate comes from $12.80 divided by 1,000. Recording the reason for an extra take helps me catch problems I could fix in the script next time.

At the end of the week, I total the credits and count the posts I actually used. I also check the payment itself, including any unused balance. That leaves me with two useful numbers: what each generation consumed and what I spent to get the posts published.

For the standard short paid clip, my starting estimate remains 300 credits, or about $3.8–4 at the $12.80/1,000 monthly pool rate. I leave room for revisions before deciding how many clips I can afford.
